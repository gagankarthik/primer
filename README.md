# The Primer

**A tutor for children aged 5 to 11 that never hands over the answer.**

Most homework help is a vending machine. A child types a question, an answer
comes out, the worksheet gets finished, and nothing is learned. The Primer does
the opposite. It answers a question with a better question, and keeps asking
until the child gets there themselves.

---

## The idea

Ask a six-year-old what seven take away three is and there are two ways to
help. You can tell her four. Or you can ask her how many she has, get her to
count them, ask her to take three away, and let her find four herself.

The second takes four minutes instead of four seconds. It is also the only one
of the two where anything happened.

The Primer only does the second one. It walks down a ladder of six increasingly
direct hints, and it will get to a worked example if it genuinely has to, but
it never opens with one. Where a child lands on that ladder is recorded, shown
to the parent, and used to decide what to ask next time.

## Who it is for

Children between five and eleven, and the adult who set them up.

The age range is the design constraint, not a marketing bracket. A five-year-old
cannot reliably read the question, so it is read aloud and answered by voice. An
eleven-year-old finds being read to patronising, so it isn't, and they type. The
same product covers both by changing what it assumes, not by offering a mode
switch nobody would find.

## What a session looks like

Four to six minutes, one idea, one thing on screen at a time.

A module is a short loop run three or four times: teach one idea, check it, and
then either move on or go back. A wrong answer never restarts the module and
never ends it. It rewinds to the idea it came from, which is then re-taught with
different words and a different prop, and asks the same thing again. Being sent
back to the beginning for one wrong answer is a punishment, and children read it
as one.

There is no score at the end. The completion screen says what the child can now
do, not how many they got wrong, because a score turns a second attempt into a
worse outcome than a first and teaches children to guess safely rather than
think.

## What a parent gets

One sentence a week, in plain English, with the evidence underneath it.

> Nell asked to just be told the answer three times this week, all of them on
> blending sounds into words. She got there each time, but she needed more help
> than usual to do it.

Under that: how much help each session took, plotted over time; every topic and
where it stands; and the full transcript of anything you want to read, both
sides, in order, with nothing summarised away.

Four numbers, chosen on one test — if this moved, would you do something
different this week?

- **Help needed**, averaged, with the direction of travel
- **Doing it independently**, the count of topics finished from a bare question
- **Got stuck, carried on**, which is the behaviour you are actually buying
- **Could use a hand**, which is where sitting next to them would help most

There is no total questions answered, no lifetime minutes, and no streak.

## What it will not do

Some of these cost us sign-ups. They are here on purpose.

**It will not just tell them.** Parents ask for this in the first fortnight.
The answer is no, and the dashboard shows the week the asking stopped.

**Nothing rewards showing up.** Every badge is for a behaviour: got stuck and
carried on, asked why unprompted, caught their own mistake. There is no badge
for minutes spent, days in a row, or questions answered. A child who learns to
farm a streak has learned the wrong lesson from a product whose entire thesis is
that shortcuts hollow you out.

**Rankings measure effort, not attainment.** The board counts badges, comebacks,
asking why and self-correcting. It does not count age, speed, time on task, or
how many were right first time, which means the child who finds everything hard
and keeps coming back can lead it. On an attainment board that child loses every
week for years and draws a permanent conclusion from it.

**No child ever loses ground.** Ranks cannot be taken away. Nothing decays,
expires, or resets on a missed day. A ladder you can fall down teaches a child
to protect a number instead of to think, and it punishes the week they were ill.

**It is slower than a flashcard app.** That is the product.

## Voice, and what we keep

Speech is turned into text as it arrives and the audio is destroyed in the same
request. **No voiceprint is ever created or stored.** This is a contract term,
not a setting, so it cannot be switched on by an update.

Nothing a child says trains a model. There is no setting for this either,
because there is nothing to decide.

A parent can read every session, export everything, and delete everything, from
their own dashboard, without asking us.

## Who controls it

The adult. Always.

The account belongs to a parent or guardian, who sets the hours it opens, the
daily cap, the PIN, and which modules are kept out of the way. A child signs in
on the same page with a four-character code from that dashboard, never with an
email address and never with a password. Children cannot change their own
limits, because a screen that lets a seven-year-old negotiate turns every
evening into a negotiation.

Outside the set hours the Primer tells the child it is closed, rather than
pretending to be broken. Children work out that a broken app might work if you
keep trying.

## Where the name comes from

Neal Stephenson's *The Diamond Age*, in which a young girl is raised by an
illustrated book that adapts to her. That book was one of a kind and cost a
fortune. This is an honest attempt at the cheap version.

One-to-one teaching has been the best education available since Aristotle, for
almost nobody. This is an attempt at closing some of that gap.
